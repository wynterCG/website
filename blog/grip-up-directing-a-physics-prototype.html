GRIP UP: directing a physics climbing prototype

Oct 21, 2026 · ~6 min read

GRIP UP is a climbing prototype in Godot 4.7 where each mouse button is one hand. Hold to grip, let go to release. The climber is a real physics body on Jolt, and the arms pull with limited strength, so swings, catches and launches come out of the simulation instead of canned animations.

AI agents built it. My job was deciding what it should be, and stopping it from turning into something else. Over sixteen versioned specs, that turned out to be most of the work.

Source on GitHub

Design pillars

Every version is judged against the same six rules:

Physics produces motion.
Never teleport the climber or inject speed to fake a move.
Player intent is simple.
Mouse and keyboard, no control over every joint.
Failure is legible.
You should know if a catch failed because of reach, speed or load.
Predictable, not trivial.
Small tolerances fix input ambiguity, never physics.
The world pushes back.
Moving structures feel your weight.
Preserve feel.
Every change is testable, measurable and reversible.

Calls I made

The pillars only matter if you act on them. A few of the turning points:

SituationCall
The physical ledge climb kept failingStop tuning the motor and add the missing physics instead: palm support and leg clearance.
The working top-out looked like a vaultRefine it, don't build a second system. One transition framework, one safety check.
The original design had no walking bodyAdd a biped, hold-to-grip and ground walking. I changed the scope on purpose.
The tuning lab could train an AI to climbDon't. Tune a few safe parameters and let people judge the feel.

Experiments, not opinions

Every new control idea ships behind a switch so it can be compared with the old one. Playtest sessions follow a written protocol: same route, same settings, three runs per variant, then ratings from 1 to 5 for precision, comfort, agency, fairness and the urge to retry. Failed runs count too.

Some ideas lost. One movement variant made uneven and twisting moves worse and was marked "revise", not shipped. That's the point of measuring: a good-sounding idea doesn't get in because it sounds good.

A lesson I'm applying now: automated checks can prove the physics is safe. They can't prove it's fun. Only people playing can do that.

What's next

The newest course, First Ascent, joins walking, wall climbing, hanging traverses and the top-out into one 30 to 90 second climb. Next step: real players on it, with the rating form, before any more tuning.
